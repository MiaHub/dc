"A healthy body naturally wants to move" is easy to write and easy to leave abstract.
These are the notes on what moving actually looks like when the day job is sitting down.

### The principles that matter

**Progressive overload.** You get stronger by gradually asking for more — more weight, more
repetitions, better control. Doing the same thing at the same effort forever maintains; it
does not build. This is the whole idea, and everything else is detail.

**Compound movements first.** A handful of patterns cover most of the body: push, pull,
squat, hinge, carry. Train those and the small muscles get worked as a consequence. Train
only the small ones and you spend a lot of time for very little.

**Consistency beats intensity.** Two sessions a week for a year beats five sessions a week
for a month followed by nothing. The programme you actually do is better than the optimal
one you abandon — the same argument as habits over motivation in
[DailyRoutine]({{ site.baseurl }}/2023/01/02/dailyroutine.html).

**Recovery is when the adaptation happens.** The session is the stimulus; the growth happens
afterwards, which is why [sleep]({{ site.baseurl }}/2025/01/12/Sleep.html) belongs in a
training note.

### Why a desk worker specifically needs this

Sitting shortens the front and weakens the back. The pattern is predictable: tight hips and
chest, weak glutes and upper back, a neck that has been holding a head forward for years.

So the priority is not symmetrical. Pulling gets more attention than pushing, hips and glutes
more than arms, and anything that opens the chest and extends the spine is worth more per
minute than another set of curls.

### Structure I keep coming back to

- Warm up properly — the older I get the less optional this is
- Two to three sessions a week, full body rather than split
- Leave a rep or two in reserve on most sets; go hard rarely and deliberately
- Write down what you lifted. You will not remember, and if you do not track it you cannot
  progress it
- When something hurts in a joint rather than a muscle, stop. That is information, not weakness

### Tennis is not a training programme

[Tennis]({{ site.baseurl }}/2025/06/15/Tennis.html) is the sport I actually enjoy, but it is
one-sided, explosive, and hard on shoulders, elbows and knees. It is a reason to be strong,
not a substitute for getting strong.

### The honest part

The logging is the habit I break first. Everything else survives a busy quarter; the notebook
is what quietly stops. And without it, six months later I am lifting exactly what I was
lifting before — which is the definition of maintaining instead of building.
